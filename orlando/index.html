How Orlando Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Equipment age matters just as much. With regular service, a tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like rust-colored water, popping noises, or moisture under the tank, it's smart to start planning a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule is cheaper and causes far less stress than an emergency swap after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Orlando

Around Orlando, water heater trouble tends to come from a handful of causes. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A standard tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod has often been used up, leaving the steel exposed to corrosion. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Homes on private wells can bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a different rod can fix. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and earthquake straps in areas that require them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
