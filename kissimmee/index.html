Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Kissimmee

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year if their water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role. With regular maintenance, a standard tank typically lasts 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like rust-colored water, rumbling, or a damp spot around the base, it's smart to start planning a replacement. A planned replacement costs less and causes far less stress than an emergency swap after a leak.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet the same sediment, ignored through a few more years, can create hot spots on the tank floor and shorten the life of the tank, forcing a replacement much sooner. Save our number on your phone now so you can reach us fast the moment something sounds or looks wrong. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Around Kissimmee, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Aging equipment is the other half of the story. A tank-style water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod has often been eaten away, leaving the steel exposed to corrosion. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: an expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that occasionally shows up when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
