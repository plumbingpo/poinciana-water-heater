Keeping Your Winter Haven Water Heater Running Longer

A little routine care is the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode rod check every three to five years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and yearly descaling for any tankless unit on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

How old is your water heater? The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the month and year it was built, and that date says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is beyond that point and is also showing discolored water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Hot Water Headaches Winter Haven Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Winter Haven face a familiar set of problems. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Many homes in the area still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and beyond the ten-year mark, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust begins attacking the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Well water can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak could damage finished space, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early.
